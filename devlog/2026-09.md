- [x] **The campaign can now be lost, and hordes stopped multiplying into confetti (2026-09-07).** Started as "hordes that seek the colony" — the largest gap named in `HordeManager`'s own header, and the thing I had told the user was the single biggest distance between this project and *They Are Billions*. **The premise was wrong, and measuring it first is the only reason that was found before code was written.**

  **What the measurement said.** `scripts/test/diagnose_horde_contact.gd` (new) runs the real map, real managers, no player action, and asks one question: do hordes ever actually reach the player. They do. A horde is within 2 hexes of a player building on **day 4**, damages one the same day, and **every player building is ruined by day 13**. Nothing needed to be made more aggressive; adding deliberate seeking would have made an already-lethal opening lethal sooner.

  **What it found instead.** The same run showed the horde count going **3 → 1,092 by day 15** while the zombies inside them only went **33 → 25,658**. Mean horde size settled at **23.5**, a hair above `SPLIT_MIN_SIZE` — the exact equilibrium unbounded splitting predicts. `MERGE_CHANCE_PER_TICK` (0.1) and `SPLIT_CHANCE_PER_TICK` (0.05) read as per-day probabilities but were rolled once per `LOGIC_TICK_SECONDS` (20 s) against a `DAY_LENGTH_SECONDS` of 2400 — **a factor of 120**, making the "5% chance" **99.8% per day**. Merging could never counterbalance it: a merge needs two hordes on the same hex out of ~4,692, and `_check_merges()` combines at most one pair per hex per tick.

  This matters beyond tidiness. `vision.md` P5 asks for "TABs on an absolutely massive scale" and the mechanic guaranteed the opposite — the threat arriving as an ever-growing confetti of 20-strong fragments instead of as a horde — while every one of those hordes replanned against `HordeFlowField` on its own.

  **Fixed** by reading both constants in the unit they were written in: `MERGE_CHANCE_PER_DAY`/`SPLIT_CHANCE_PER_DAY`, same values, converted by `per_tick_chance()` as `1 - (1-p)^(1/ticks_per_day)`. Not `p/ticks` — that is close for small p and wrong as p rises, and a certainty must convert to a certainty per tick rather than to 1/120. Measured on the same map, same seed, same day: **1,092 hordes → 33**, holding 25,894 zombies against 25,658. Damage events over the run **41 → 3**, and the colony still had a building standing past day 25 where before it was gone by day 13. On the gate's own fixture, one 4,000-strong horde becomes **293 hordes in 20 days before, 6 after**. (D74)

  **The campaign can now end.** `vision.md` P4 — "it must be able to happen or nothing is at stake" — had no implementation at all; the only loss concept in the game was `TerritoryController.is_lost(coord)`, which is one hex flipping contested. `DefeatConditionMonitor` (new, `scripts/campaign/`) implements `backlog.md` 7.6's already-settled condition verbatim: all three of nothing affordable, nothing produced, and no standing trainer, **simultaneously**. It emits signals and `EventManager` raises the event, so `AlertManager`'s existing auto-pause-above-INFO rule stops the clock — no new pause logic anywhere. Three readings had to be settled and are recorded in D73 rather than left to be re-derived, the load-bearing one being an **arming latch**: between `_ready()` and `seed_starting_buildings()` the player owns nothing, so all three conditions hold and every campaign would otherwise end on day one.

  **Gated, and the gates were mutation-tested rather than trusted.** `verify_defeat_condition.gd` (7 checks) and `verify_horde_fragmentation.gd` (3 checks), both new. Four mutations were run against them; **one survived**, and that was worth more than the three that did not: deleting `evaluate_now()`'s `if _defeated: return` latch left every check passing, because a REBUILT colony satisfies no conditions and so would not have re-emitted anyway. The check was testing the wrong half. It now re-enters the losing state after a loss and asserts `campaign_lost` does not fire twice, which the same mutation fails.

  **Two harness traps, both of which produced a confidently wrong answer first (D75).** `--headless` never focuses its root window, so `BackgroundExecutionManager` pins `Engine.max_fps` to 15 and **any awaited-frame loop is bound to real time** — the first version of the diagnostic spent over 20 real minutes on 15 simulated days at 6% CPU. This is D72's `max_fps` trap arriving by a second route; the fix is to drive `_process()` at a fixed delta, which is deterministic as well as fast. And **GDScript lambdas capture locals by value**, so a signal handler counting into a local reports zero forever — the fragmentation gate's vacuity check claimed splitting was dead while the check directly above it was watching it work.

  **Filed, not fixed.** Walls-block-bleed was retagged `[gated]` → `[design]`: `InfestationManager` implements **no hex-to-hex spread at all** (`run_daily_tick()` breeds in place and `export_from()` spawns the horde on the source hex), and walls deliberately do not stop hordes (D17), so there is no passive bleed for a wall to reduce by 40%. §2.1 reads both ways and the user has to pick. The undefended colony dying inside a fortnight was also filed as `[design]` rather than tuned — that is the "how hard is the opening" question, and it belongs with the escalation item that asks it from the other end.

- [x] **Tactical view ran at 20 fps because of the minimap (2026-09-07).** User report: "when in tactical view the game is very very slow and laggy." **Every hypothesis reached by reading the code was wrong**, and the only reason the real cause was found is that nothing was changed until it was profiled.

  **What the profile said.** `scripts/test/profile_tactical.gd` (new, windowed — a headless viewport renders nothing, so this class of problem is invisible to it) plus `profile_tactical_bisect.gd` (new): 48.03 ms/frame, 20.8 fps, flat across all three Tactical zoom bands. An empty scene on the same machine, same window, same settings: **0.22 ms**. Coarse bisection:

  | state | med ms | recovered |
  | --- | ---: | ---: |
  | as shipped | 48.03 | — |
  | WorldRoot hidden | 45.87 | **2.16** |
  | MainHUD hidden | 6.23 | **41.80** |
  | every `_process` off, whole tree | 43.99 | 4.04 |
  | autoloads' `_process` off | 47.78 | 0.25 |

  **The entire simulated world — terrain, streaming, 60,000 individual zombies — cost 2.16 ms. The HUD cost 41.80 ms.**

  **What it was.** `MinimapView._draw()` walked all **27,566 generated cells twice per frame**, once asking `FogOfWarManager.get_fog_state()` and once asking `NoiseManager.get_noise_at()`, building a 6-corner polygon per hex, into a **200x237 pixel panel** — and did it on **every frame** (measured: 200 draws in 200 frames) rather than at the 10 Hz its own `VIEWPORT_REFRESH_SECONDS` timer intends, because every content signal called `queue_redraw()` directly. The threat-marker half alone was 17.7 ms. Its own comment defended the approach: "cheap at this scale ... `get_noise_at()` is already a plain Dictionary lookup." Each lookup is cheap.

  **Fixed** by giving both managers an iterator over their own sparse field (D70) and coalescing redraws onto the existing timer (D71). **48.03 ms -> 6.51 ms, 20.8 fps -> 153.6 fps.** The HUD went 41.80 ms -> 0.32 ms.

  **The gate caught the first version of the fix being useless.** `get_known_hexes()` returned `_fog_state.keys()`, reasoning that an absent hex reads UNSEEN and so was never drawn — true, and worthless, because `FogOfWarManager._ready()` seeds an UNSEEN entry for **every** generated cell to push each `HexCellView`'s initial darkness. The set was 100% of the map. `verify_sparse_field_iteration.gd`'s third check — "are the sparse sets actually sparse" — is what failed, and it exists precisely because checks 1 and 2 (equivalence) pass happily when both sides scan the whole map. Now an incremental `_explored` index: fog 12%, noise 6% of cells in the fixture.

  **Verified visually, not just numerically.** The minimap is a render, so `smoke_screenshot.gd` was run before and after and the panel diffed per pixel: **0 of 40,376 pixels differ** across all three Tactical framings.

  **Three wrong answers worth recording, because each looked right.**
  - **Chunk builds.** Real — `bench_chunk_build.gd` (new) measures **61.7 ms mean per chunk, 3.7 frames**, worst 359 ms — but not the steady-state cost. Filed in `backlog.md`.
  - **The 18,769 `HexCellView` nodes.** `HexGridMap` builds four nodes per land hex and its own comment notes nothing ever hides them at Tactical zoom, where `TerrainMeshView` has taken over. Removing all of them from the tree recovered **1.60 ms**.
  - **"48,000 iterations" in the prop-bucketing loop.** That is `MAX_PROPS_PER_CHUNK`, not reality: real chunks average **2,147 props** and the whole loop is 0.5 ms. The eager-`[]`-default waste is real and worth 46% of it — of half a millisecond.

  **Two profiling traps, both of which produced confidently wrong answers (D72).** `CanvasLayer` is not a `CanvasItem`, so an ablation guarded on `node is CanvasItem` never hid `MainHUD` and reported the most expensive node in the game at **-0.17 ms** — that one sweep sent the whole investigation down a blind alley. And `BackgroundExecutionManager` pins `Engine.max_fps = 15` on `focus_exited`, so an unfocused profiler window reads 66.66 ms on every row of a sweep; `Engine.max_fps = 0` has to be re-asserted inside the sample loop, not once at startup.

- [x] **Noise emission: a flat 2-hex disc becomes a decibel (2026-09-01).** `backlog.md` asked for "§6's per-source dB and attenuation model" in place of `NoiseManager`'s flat aura, which it measured at ~43x the reach of §6's loudest listed sound. **Taking that literally would have deleted the mechanic**, and finding that out first is what shaped the whole change.

  **The measurement that reframed the item.** A tactical tile is 10 m (design_doc.md §7) and a macro hex is 8,647 m centre to centre. So §6's whole sound table fits inside a twentieth of one hex: melee 20 m, construction 80 m, steam engine 120 m, rifle 150 m, Maxim 250 m, artillery 400 m. `HordeManager`'s ATTRACTED state scans whole hexes and paths toward the loudest, so emission at §6's radii never crosses a hex boundary and no horde is ever drawn to anything — which is the counterplay `vision.md` P2 calls the core tension engine of the game. §6 has no strategic row to use instead; its only building entry is "Building Construction, 8 tiles". So what shipped is §6's **model** — a source level in dB at 10 m, geometric spreading, air absorption, and §6's woodland/high-ground/mountain rules — with buildings on their own scale, and the table left for the tactical consumer that does not exist yet (D66).

  **`noise_output` (a 0-6 rank) became `noise_source_db` (a real level at 10 m), 80 to 108 across 17 buildings.** The old field's own comment already wanted this — "a Foundry's hammering is louder than a Brickworks' kiln" — and a rank could not express it: every building above 0 projected the *identical* 2-hex disc and differed only in magnitude, uniformly, at every hex inside it. Now reach is derived, and it spans **0.85 hexes for a Brickworks against 2.03 for a Bessemer Smelting Complex** (D67). The loudest is calibrated to land where the flat disc already reached, so this is a change of shape and not of balance — `NoisePropagation.HEARING_THRESHOLD_DB` is the single knob that decides it, and `verify_noise_emission.gd` fails if it drifts.

  **Sources combine as intensities, not as sums (D68).** The old code added contributions and its comment claimed that was "closer to how real industrial noise stacks." It is the opposite: levels are logarithmic and intensities add, so four equal buildings on one hex are +6 dB, not 4x. A ten-building district used to read as ten times as attractive; it now reads as 10 dB louder. Accepted cost: stacking industry is a much smaller penalty than it was, which the per-source reach spread more than replaces as a decision.

  **Two things the performance measurement killed, both of them my first answer.**

  1. **Ray-marching each source/listener path.** 250 m samples through `SubHexTerrainQuery`, cached per hex pair. At 200 buildings a recompute took **55 s and never got faster** — 7,400 pairs at ~70 samples each both overran the path cache and thrashed it, so warm never happened. Sampling each **hex's** own terrain once and walking the hex line between endpoints costs one dictionary lookup per hex crossed: **0.23 s cold, 39 ms warm.** `recompute()` runs on every building placed, so the first version was a hitch the player would have felt on every click.
  2. **Reading elevation through `SubHexTerrainQuery.elevation_metres()`,** the fine 30 m path `CLAUDE.md` §3 names as sanctioned. It loads one ~27 KB PNG per hex, and a 200-building colony needs 360 of them: with only that line changed, a cold recompute went **0.23 s → 11.5 s**. Measured the error the coarse raster costs instead of assuming it: over 140 real corridor hexes the high-ground FRACTION differs by 0.057 on average and 0.33 at worst, which is **0.34 dB mean and 2.0 dB worst-case** once multiplied into the 6 dB rule, against sources spanning 80-108 dB. Taken, and said out loud in the code rather than built on quietly, which is what §3 asks for. What makes it defensible rather than merely cheap: the quantity is a SHARE of a 65 km² hex used as a share of a kilometres-long path integral, and `RealTerrainSampler` makes the same call for the same reason — the fine bake deliberately covers biome and terrain_feature and not elevation, because "elevation varies smoothly enough at this scale". Biome, where sub-hex detail is real and cheap, still reads the fine tile. (D69)

  **A third thing the measurement caught, and this one was a design error rather than a cost one.** §6 says "Level 2/3 Highlands reduce sound propagation radius by 50%", which is exactly 6 dB under spreading. The first version applied it whole the moment a path touched any high ground — and `ElevationLevels` puts HILL at 100 m, which most of northern England clears. So it fired on essentially every path around the starting corridor and became a global -6 dB rather than a property of a barrier: an Iron Foundry put 18.0 attraction on **every** neighbouring hex in **every** direction where a clear path gives 24.0. Now scaled by the share of the path that is actually high ground.

  **What the player gets, over real ground rather than on paper** (`scripts/test/diagnose_noise_emission.gd`, mid-corridor at hex 80,120): the terrain there costs a mean 8.0 dB to a neighbouring hex and 12.2 dB at worst, so the loudest building pulls **1.67 hexes through that ground against 2.03 on a clear path — 82%**. A Brickworks lights its own hex and nothing else. Building the works behind a ridge is now a real decision, and a Level 4 mountain blocks the path outright.

  **Gated by `scripts/test/verify_noise_emission.gd`,** six checks, and mutation-tested rather than assumed: make the field flat again by ignoring distance → 3 failures; make terrain never attenuate → 1; decalibrate `HEARING_THRESHOLD_DB` from 5.0 to 30.0 → 2; sum levels linearly instead of intensities → 1, reporting the 323.9 gain against the 6.0 that is due. Unmutated, it passes. The first mutation I wrote for the intensity check was a no-op (an intensity → level → intensity round trip) and passed — worth recording, because a mutation that does not change behaviour proves nothing about the gate.

  **Two existing gates assert on the old model and were updated rather than relaxed.** `verify_building_power.gd`'s "emits no noise" check and `verify_building_state_emissions.gd`'s Watchtower expectations both computed their expected values from `noise_output` and `NIGHT_NOISE_MULTIPLIER`; both now derive them through `NoisePropagation`, so a balance pass moves them instead of turning them into false failures. Night itself changed shape: it used to double the field, which is meaningless applied to a decibel, and now adds 6 dB to the source — one doubling of audible **distance**, which is what "night carries further" should mean, and which sound genuinely does after dark under a temperature inversion.

  **Also re-derived: `NoiseVisuals.VISUALIZATION_MAX_NOISE`, 12.0 → 30.0.** The field is no longer a 0-12 sum. The loudest building puts 103 on its own hex, 27 one hex out and 3.7 two hexes out, so normalising against its peak would paint every hex but the source amber; 30 is the near-ring value of a heavy works, which is where the Threat Meter should saturate.
- [x] **The sub-hex terrain cache was costing 851 bytes a sub-cell and never giving one back (2026-09-01).** `SubHexTerrainQuery._cache` was unbounded, static, and keyed by a `"%d_%d_%d_%d"` String built on every call, hit or miss. The backlog named all three properties and proposed one fix — a FIFO cap like `HordeFlowField.MAX_CACHED_FIELDS`. Measuring first (`scripts/test/bench_subhex_cache.gd`, new) changed both which property mattered and which fix to use.

  **The measurement.** 400,000 distinct sub-cells — **under four hexes' worth**, against a corridor of 3,876 — cost **324.7 MB resident, 851 bytes per entry**, and stayed for the process lifetime. A warm lookup cost **2.06 us**. The String key was the obvious defect and the *minority* of the cost: each entry was its own four-field Dictionary, and Godot's Dictionary is a heap-allocated HashMap whichever four fields you put in it.

  **What the bake actually holds decided the fix.** **985 distinct terrain values across all 2,661,336 sub-cells of 24 real corridor hexes** — every single one of them, not a sample. The elevation raster is written by `bake_landcover.py` at `step = 4` with a nearest-neighbour upsample, so a hex holds ~15 distinct elevations however finely it is read, and biome/terrain_feature come from a small enum each. So the sample Dictionaries are **interned**: one shared instance per distinct VALUE, replacing one per ADDRESS. Sampling the whole corridor at every 15th sub-cell finds 4,190 distinct values and 785 distinct elevations, and full density finds 1.47x what stride 15 does on the same ground — so the corridor's full-density palette is order 6,000, about a tenth of `MAX_INTERNED_SAMPLES`.

  **Result: 324.7 MB → 11.0 MB, 851 → 84 bytes per live entry, 2.06 us → 0.91 us warm** (the last measured on identical benchmark code against both versions, since the two classes cannot run the same bench). Hard ceiling now 2 x 131,072 addresses x 84 B = **21.0 MB**.

  **Three parts, one per cost.** The address is a packed 60-bit int (21 biased bits per axial coordinate, 9 per sub index — a sub index is `clampi`-ed to 0..332 at both entry points, and the map is 154x179). The sample is interned. The address map is bounded by a **two-generation rollover** rather than a FIFO: at the cap the hot map becomes the cold one, a fresh hot map starts, the old cold map is dropped whole, and a cold hit *moves* back into hot. A FIFO order list is right at `HordeFlowField`'s 64 entries and wrong at 131,072, where it would itself be a six-figure Array of keys — sized to the problem it exists to bound.

  **The cap has a floor, not just a ceiling.** One hex's address space is `SUB_HEX_GRID_N^2` = 110,889 sub-cells. Below that a full-hex sweep would roll the generation over on itself and re-sample ground it had just read, so 131,072 is chosen against 110,889 and not against the hexagon's 65 km² — the addressable square is not the hexagon, and that distinction is exactly what a later "trim it to 0.75x, it is still bigger than a hex" would get wrong.

  **Interning changes what a mutation costs, and that is the one thing gated hardest.** `sample()` already returned the cached Dictionary by reference and the urban override has always been applied to a duplicate — so interning weakens no existing guarantee. What it does is widen the blast radius: two sub-cells in *different hexes* over identical terrain now share one instance, so an override written in place would repaint terrain in a hex nobody founded anything in. `scripts/test/verify_subhex_cache.gd` (new, 8.9 s) finds a genuine cross-hex pair in the fixture region, paves one sub-cell of one of them with a disc smaller than a sub-cell, and asserts the other is untouched — plus that clearing the disc restores the paved cell's baked biome.

  **The gate was mutation-tested rather than assumed to work.** Remove the `duplicate()` in `_with_urban_override` → 2 failures. Make `_remember()` never roll over → 1 failure. Make `_address_key()` drop the `hex.y` field → 1 failure. Unmutated → passes. Its four checks are: the cap holds under a flood of never-revisited addresses; a fixture answer read before a flood is identical after it (the flood deliberately walks a *disjoint* set of hexes, or several fixture addresses would survive in the cold generation and be compared against themselves); every fixture address agrees with `RealTerrainSampler` directly, which is how a key collision would surface; and the urban-override pair above.

  **One real bug came out of reviewing this, in the first version of the fix itself:** the cold-to-hot promotion inserted into hot without erasing from cold, so a promoted address lived in both maps and `cache_size()` — documented as a live address count, and divided by workload size in `bench_portal_blocking.gd` — double-counted it. Promotion now moves rather than copies.

  **Two downstream comments were made false by this and are corrected in place.** `bench_portal_blocking.gd`'s stated question was "how many cache entries a real routing session strands in a static Dictionary **that is never evicted**", and its corridor extrapolation printed an unbounded entry count; it now reports that figure as a percentage of the cache's own ceiling (a full corridor sweep touches 13,002 sub-cells, 5% of it), because past the ceiling the number stops being a memory figure and becomes a churn one. And `backlog.md`'s vector-terrain phase 2 argues its point-in-polygon cost away with "runs once per cell ever touched" — no longer true, so its per-hex polygon bucket index stops being a mitigation and becomes a requirement.

  **Process note.** The adversarial review of this change ran as a multi-agent workflow and **39 of its 41 agents died on a session limit**, which auto-marked every finding "refuted" through a scoring bug in the harness script — `survived` was computed as "fewer than 2 of the verifiers refuted it", and zero surviving verifiers satisfies that vacuously in the wrong direction. The two lenses that did complete produced twelve findings, of which eleven were real and are fixed above. A verification result derived from agents that never ran is not a verification result; read the raw findings.
